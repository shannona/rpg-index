Dagon Index

Version 1.00: Issues 1-27

by Shannon Appelcline

Overview

History: 1983-1989

Carl T. Ford, who would found Call of Cthulhu's first fazine, Dagon (1983-1990), was fascinating by "the weird and the mysterious" from a young age. He even owned a copy of A Pictorial History of Horror Movies (1958), but by the age of 10 still hadn't gotten to watch one! That's when he petitioned to watch The Revenge of Frankenstein (1958), which was showing on ITV. He was confused why the monster didn't look like the one in his book (which depicted Boris Karloff as the monster), but he nonetheless enjoyed the experience. Dracula Has Risen from the Grave (1968) followed a few months later, and with it came a love of Hammer films.

Horror movie magazines followed and then the works of H.P. Lovecraft, which arrived in two volumes: H.P. Lovecraft's The Case of Charles Dexter Ward (Panther edition, 1963) and August Derleth's The Mask of Cthulhu (Consul edition, 1961). Though Lovecraft's stories were often hard to find in the United States in the decades after his death, a larger set of paperbacks was available for a time in the United Kingdom.

Inevitably, Chaosium's Call of Cthulhu (1981) slithered into Ford's life as well. His first experience here was courtesy of Mark Buckley, who ran an adventure called "Brimstone at the Club of the Black Arts." Ford played a journalist investigating the disappearance of another writer, who'd been helping to research a book on cults. (Ultimately, the cults were a red herring, with the real monster being of a different sort entirely.)

For Players of 'CoC': 1983-1986

In November 1983, Ford, then at college in Middlesex in the UK, decided to publish a "non-profit fan module" of his own. Call of Cthulhu at the time was still a young endeavor, having been published by Chaosium exactly two years' previous, on November 13, 1981. However, a handful of adventures had already appeared. Chaosium had published Shadows of Yog-Sothoth (1982) and The Asylum & Other Tales (1983), while Theatre of the Mind Enterprises (TOME) had produced three adventures that year, The Arkham Evil (1983), Death in Dunwich (1983), and Pursuit to Kadath (1983).

All five adventures were available through Games Workshop in the UK, but after being imported they cost £7 or £8: the equivalent of $30-$38 in modern US dollars. In addition, five adventures over the course of two years wasn't a lot (even when you added in a few more magazine adventures)! Ford decided that he wanted to "fill the gaps of boredom" between those official (and licensed) publications, and to simultaneously offer something cheaper, so he wrote, typed, photocopied, stapled and printed an A4 'zine (approximately letter sized) containing a 1930s-era adventure set in Lovecraft Country called "No Room at Innsmouth",with cover and interior art by Nick Basi. Because the work was all volunteer, Ford was able to offer it for the price of a mere £0.55—though he'd later comment that the cardstock covers alone cost £0.22, which would make it hard to maintain the price. (He would jump to £0.85 only a year and a half later, but would never ask for more than £1.50 for his single issues.) Still, Ford might have printed as few as ten copies for his initial print run, for friends and "a couple of the guys in the original Dalling Road branch of Games Workshop."

Though Ford's initial release consisted of a single adventure (and a short "editorial" explaining it), he published it as Dagon #1 (November 1983) and called it a "fanzine-come-module." The other shoe dropped with Dagon #2 (February 1984), which not only included the second part of Ford's adventure, "The Lighthouse," but also a comic adaptation of H.P. Lovecraft's "The Cats of Ulthar" and a review of Chaosium's Cthulhu Companion (1983). Then Dagon #3 (August 1984), which closed out "No Room at Innsmouth" with "Devil Reef," also included an adaptation of Lovecraft's "The Festival" alongside two articles by other people: a review of Lovecraft's "The Call of Cthulhu" (1928) by Ford's first Call of Cthulhu GM, Mark Buckley, and an article on gun laws in Britain by Karl Greenwood.

Dagon was becoming a true 'zine (and therefore the first Call of Cthulhu 'zine), not just a serially printed scenario. Ford had originally planned it as an eight-times-a-year publication, but the six-month gap between the second and third issues quickly proved that wasn't viable. In fact, Ford had folded the magazine between those issues because of an inability to distribute it: he'd lost money on the first two issues, and worse very few people had read his work. He only gave it another go in the summer of 1984 after he finished exams and had free time.

Fortunately, on his second go-around, Ford was able to offer quick answers to the distribution dilemma. That started at Games Day '84 in September. Ford not only sold a few issues there himself, but was also able to place 50 at the "fanzine stand." From there, he was headed ever upward. By Dagon #6 (March/April 1985), Ford was having troubles obtaining the cardstock covers in sufficient quantities due to the increased demand, which resulted in a change to A5 (digest-sized) with Dagon #7 (May/June 1985). Meanwhile, the back issues had all sold out, while Ford was also increasing his print run to 250. This growth was likely due to not just Ford's improved penetration into distribution, but also Games Workshop production of a locally printed copy of Call of Cthulhu Second Edition (1984), which dropped the game's price in the UK from £18.95 to £9.95 as of the ads in White Dwarf #54 (June 1984), and later their creation of an attractive single-volume hardcover Call of Cthulhu Third Edition (1985).

The first six issues of Dagon were focused on singular scenarios, notably including the adventure that got Ford started, Mark Buckley's "Brimstone at the Club of the Black Arts," which appeared in Dagon #4 (October 1984). There was additional content, but it was still just several pieces in each issue, and there wasn't even a table of contents to locate them! Dagon #7 marked an evolution of the magazine, not just in its move to A5, but also its publication of over a dozen articles, including new columns such as a 'zine news column called "Dr. Phibes Prescribes" and a letters column called "Black Mail," which quickly became as active as an APA.

There was also an ever-growing cast of game writers, the most notable of which was certainly Mark Morrison, not just because of his contributions of Keeper advice and adventures to the 'zine over the following years, but also because he became the official distributor of the magazine in Australia starting with Dagon #10 (November/December, 1985). By 1987, Dagon was also being imported into Canada by Soft Books and into the United States by Bob Weinberg.

One other sign revealed the growing success of Dagon: at Games Day '85 it won the award for "Best Amateur Games Magazine," a feat that it would repeat at Games Day 86—but Ford wouldn't be there to collect his second award, because he was instead at Fantasycon XI (1985): by that time, he was seeing other hobbies and Dagon was seeing other content.

For Readers of the Cthulhu Mythos: 1986-1990

When Dagon debuted, it was labeled as being "For FRPs of 'C. of C.'." Over time, that evolved to instead read "For players of CoC," a subtitle that it held through Dagon #11 (January 1986). With Dagon #12 (April 1986), however, the subtitle changed to "For Readers of the Cthulhu Mythos and players of 'Call of Cthulhu", which it used until Dagon #21 (March-May 1988), at which point it removed its subtitle entirely.

This represented a real change in the magazine, but one that was gradual in the issues leading up to Dagon #11. In fact, Dagon had played with Mythos fiction as far back as Dagon #4, which included a short piece that Ford himself had written called "The Statement." But things took a turn with Dagon #8 (July 1985) when Ford began publishing new work by a small-press Mythos fiction writer named Robert J. Curran, who had previously published Mythos fiction for a variety of fanzines, including ones that are largely unknown today such as Weird Window (1970-1971) and Balthus (1971-1972) as well as Gordon Linzner's Space and Time (1966-Present).

Two Curran stories were serialized over a total of five issues, through Dagon #12, and by that time changes were starting to accrue for the magazine. Ford announced this transforming landscape in Dagon #9 (September 1985). He said that he wanted to step back from more mechanical rules-based articles and instead push into discussions of the background of the Mythos—whether it be through RPG articles or more general articles about Lovecraft's writing. This led to a new column by Peter F. Jeffery called "The Red Brain's Trust," which answered questions about Lovecraft and his fiction based on Jeffrey's expert knowledge. It debuted in Dagon #10 (November/December 1985). Other Lovecraftian scholars appeared in later issues, starting with Robert M. Price in Dagon #11.

That same issue also featured a cover by Irish artist Dave Carson, marking a notable improvement in the 'zine's graphic design. (More popular arists followed, including Allen Koszowski and Gahan Wilson.) Dagon #13 (June-September 1986), which featured a photograph instead of an illustration, then moved to a glossy cover as another upgrade.

Meanwhile, the writers were coming, and they were some of the up-and-coming names of the horror community at the time, starting with a few notables from the UK. Brian Lumley debuted in Dagon #12 with "The House of the Temple", a story that had originally run in Weird Tales #3 (1981), the third issue of Lin Carter's paperback revival of the classic title. But Ford impressively got the first UK rights for the tale. Lumley remained a friend of the magazine for years afterward and even wrote an original comic, "Return to Arkham," for Dagon #15 (November/December 1986).

Many, many more fiction stories followed, often multiple in an issue, supplemented by scholarly analysis of the authors and their stories. Today, most readers probably remember Dagon best for the extensive fiction that ran through its last several years, especially the magazine's spotlight issues: Brian Lumley in Dagon #13, T.E.D Klein in Dagon double-issue #18-19 (July-October 1987), Thomas Ligotti in Dagon #22-23 (September-December 1988), and D.F. Lewis in Dagon #26 (October 1989). As that authorial list reveals, Dagon not only moved from gaming articles to fiction and analysis but also, over time, from the Cthulhu Mythos to more general "cosmic horror," often by authors who at the time were new and untested.

Those shifting interests are almost certainly why Ford ended up at FantasyCon XI instead of Games Day '85 on the last weekend of September 1985, and so didn't personally claim his second Best Amateur Games Magazine Award. But, as Dagon's tides turned, so did its recognition. In later years, Ford would win not Games Day awards, but instead British Fantasy Awards. He personally won the Icarus award for best newcomer in 1988 and also won the Best Small Press Award for Dagon from 1988 through 1990.

Ironically, just as the fiction content was ramping up, Call of Cthulhu publisher Chaosium took an interest in Dagon. This was courtesy of some issues of Dagon that Ford gave to Chaosium founder Greg Stafford when he attended Games Day '85. As a result, Call of Cthulhu designer Sandy Petersen wrote a column called "The Acolyte," which mixed Chaosium news with his own GMing advice, running intermittently from Dagon #11 through Dagon #20 (November-January 1987).

This demonstrated that roleplaying content wasn't entirely cut from Dagon in its later days; it was just taking a back seat to the fiction and literary analysis. What did remain resulted in interesting crossovers. That started with a few adventures by Mark Morrison that were inspired by authors other than H.P. Lovecraft: "Landscrapes," inspired by T.E.D. Klein, which appeared in Dagon #18/19 and "In a City of Bells and Towers," partially inspired by Thomas Ligotti, which was published in Dagon #22/23. Both adventures were also later reused in Chaosium campaigns, "Landscrapes" in At Your Door (1990) and "In a City of Bells and Towers" in Horror on the Orient Express (1991).

The crossovers in the "Black Mail" letters column were even more intriguing, because they revealed the worlds of the Call of Cthulhu RPG and traditional Mythos fiction writers and artists crashing together for the first time. In Dagon #10, Ramsey Campbell wrote that he couldn't "pretend any interest in gaming," but that he enjoyed the literary analysis that was already beginning to infiltrate the magazine. In the next issue, Brian Lumley admitted he was a bit more knowledgeable, saying: "I'm not really into gaming, but I do know of a lot of people who are." Then, Thomas Ligotti offered the strongest praise in Dagon #21, saying "I found the game scenario very entertaining and instructive in the essentials of horror. No doubt I'm not the first reader to appreciate a game scenario as a prose screenplay for a horror movie that one is allowed to flesh out in the imagination." It wasn't the only such complementary crossover at the time: over in Rod Serling's The Twilight Zone Magazine vol. 5 #3 (July/August 1985), artist Gahan Wilson wrote an extensive article detailing three different Call of Cthulhu adventures he played, including one with staff from Grenadier Models and another with none other than Robert M. Price; however, Twilight Zone Magazine editor T.E.D. Klein was less positive, saying, "Instead of a true sense of wonder, [RPGs] offer odds and numbers.

As Ford was turning Dagon from a roleplaying 'zine (with bits of fiction) into a fiction and literary analysis 'zine (with bits of RPG content), he was also working to make Mythos fiction more available in the UK. In late 1986, he therefore founded "Dagon Press," which initially focused on importing the small-press publications of Mark A. Michaud's Necronomicon Press. At the time this included 'zines by luminaries such as S.T. Joshi and Robert M. Price as well as Lovecraft miscellania such as chapbooks of the poem cycle "Fungi from Yuggoth" (1929-1930) and the round-robin story "The Challenge from Beyond" (1935).

Later Ford also published two of his own books through Dagon Press. The first was Dave Carson's third portfolio, Haunters of the Dark and Other Lovecraftian Horrors (1987). The signed set of six black-and-white plates featured "The Whisperer in Darkness," "The Gable Window," "The Tomb Herd," "The Shambler from the Stars," "The Burrowers Beneath," and "The Hound." It was limited to 450 copies. Dagon Press's other publication was a story that Ford commissioned from Brian Lumley to mix the Mythos with roleplaying, called Synchronicity, or Something (1988). This signed-and-numbered edition was limited to 350 copies. It was long available only in the small press, until its collection two decades later in Hagoppian and Other Stories (2008), the second of two volumes prepared by Subterranean Press to highlight Lumley's "best" Cthulhu Mythos writing.

In the early days of Dagon, Ford was able to reprint copies when there was a need, but that practice fell off as the 'zine became more popular. And it definitely became popular: by the end of the run, Ford had a mailing list of 500 active subscribers, not counting copies that were being sent to distributors across the world. There were plans for Ford and Morrison to produce a "Beast of Dagon," reprinting some of the best articles from the 'zine, but it was eventually dropped because it was too difficult to find all the writers and clear rights with them.

As for the end of the run: that was Dagon #27 (June 1990), an unassuming "HPL Humor Special," the second in the 'zine's run. There, Ford announced that Dagon would be coming to an end with the 32nd issue. It would turn out to be final issue, however, with #28, a planned spotlight on Polish writer Stefan Grabinski, never appearing. Tragically, a planned book from Dagon Press called "Lovecraft: A Centennial Tribute," intended for August 1990, also never surfaced. It was supposed to be "a collection of twenty essays by twenty leading Lovecraftian scholars and authors" that Ford called "the finest item the press has put together".

The reason that Ford gave for Dagon's end was "a lack of time." Later he'd expand, noting his time and energy was being eaten up by "a string of illnesses" and "numerous nights on the London tiles and a short dalliance as vocalist for a forgettable two-chord punk band." He decided that life was too short "to sit behind a typewriter long into the early hours" and that he'd done his part for Lovecraft, which hadn't been very available in print when he got started and now was "big business." By giving up the 'zine, he was able to discover "girls, goth and clubland."

After Dagon: 1990-2017

When Dagon first appeared in early 1983, most Call of Cthulhu content was appearing from a trio of publishers: Chaosium, who obviously published the game; Games Workshop, who imported it into the UK; and Theatre of the Mind Enterprises, who'd acquired the first third-party license. Of them, TOME's work was the only sign of a wider fandom supporting the game. Most magazine publications were similarly in Chaosium's Different Worlds (1979-1987) and Games Workshop's White Dwarf (1977-Present). Though there were scattered articles in various sources such as David Nalle's Abyss magazine (1979-1984) and Tim Kask's Adventure Gaming (1981-1982), they were infrequent.

When Dagon ended publication in mid-1990, not a lot had changed. Call of Cthulhu was appearing in more professional periodicals than ever before, including mainstays such as Dragon (1976-2007), but fandom seemed to have risen and fallen. The second Call of Cthulhu zine, Polaris, had lasted a single issue. Even White Dwarf's Call of Cthulhu column, "Crawling Chaos," had vanished into the void. TOME had come and gone as well.

The few signs of continuing fandom were much more localized. One was Mark Morrison's Cthulhu Conglomerate, which ran tournaments at conventions in Australia. Another was the H.P. Lovecraft Historical Society, who had adopted Call of Cthulhu into a live-action RPG called "Cthulhu Lives!" and then published a 'zine called Strange Eons (1987-1990) to support their work. But neither was well known in the wider world of Call of Cthulhu gaming.

However, large-scale fandom was about to rebound. In August 1990, two months after Ford published the last Dagon, one John Tynes attended Gen Con '90 where he met Jeff Carey, who talked about starting a new Call of Cthulhu 'zine. Carey didn't but Tynes did. His The Unspeakable Oath (1990-2001+) would carry the torch of Call of Cthulhu fandom in the '90s just as Dagon had in the '80s (before fandom truly exploded in the 21st century).

Meanwhile, Ford himself had other hobbies after Dagon including greyhound racing (with his own greyhound!). There was then some talk of him reviving Dagon around 2003, just after the first run of The Unspeakable Oath came to an end. It never happened, reportedly due to continued ill health.

Carl T. Ford passed away in November 13, 2017.

Obtaining the Magazines

Difficulty in obtaining rights means that Dagon will likely never be reprinted or made officially available online (though several issues have unofficially made their way onto archive.org). Issues are also hard to obtain on secondary markets. The first half-dozen or more seem largely non-existent. From Dagon #13, which was the first glossy cover, they turn up more often, but seem to average about $50 each (and overall run between $40 and $100 per issue on the current market).

The issues I used to create this index (and write this history) originated with Mark Morrison, the Australian distributor of Dagon. (Thanks!)

About the Game Systems

Call of Cthulhu remained largely the same from the first edition in 1981 through the sixth edition in 2004. Game articles in this index are therefore flagged as CoC 1-6e to designate that they were for an early edition of the game, prior to the larger-scale seventh edition revision in 2014.

About the Pagination

The pages in early issues of Dagon were not numbered. This index assumes that the first page with text content was page 1 and counts from there.
